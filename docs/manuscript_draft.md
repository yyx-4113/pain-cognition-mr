# Genetically predicted multisite chronic pain is compatible with lower cognitive performance: a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study with CHARLS cohort context

**Yongxin Yang**
The Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Fuzhou, Fujian 350003, China. ORCID: 0009-0004-9698-6552.

## Abstract

**Background.** Chronic pain and cognitive impairment are two escalating public-health burdens in ageing populations. Observational studies linking multisite chronic pain (MCP) to cognitive decline are confounded by reverse causation and shared comorbidity. We used Mendelian randomization (MR) to assess the causal direction and magnitude of the association between genetically predicted MCP and cognitive outcomes, embedded in population-based descriptive correlates (US NHANES) of subjective memory complaint. NHANES contains no chronic-pain questionnaire in the analysed cycles and therefore does not test the pain→cognition hypothesis.

**Methods.** Two-sample MR was conducted with MCP (Johnston et al., 2019; UK Biobank, n=387,649) as exposure. Outcomes were cognitive performance (OpenGWAS `ebi-a-GCST006572`, UK Biobank-derived, n=257,841), Alzheimer's disease (IGAP, `ieu-a-297`, n=54,162; Ben Nevis/ADGC, `ieu-b-2`, n=63,926), and all-cause dementia (FinnGen, `finn-b-F5_DEMENTIA`, n=216,771). Independent instrumental SNPs were selected at P<5×10⁻⁸ with LD clumping (r²<0.001, 10,000 kb). Causal estimates used inverse-variance-weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, and weighted-median methods; heterogeneity (Cochran Q / I²), directional pleiotropy (Egger intercept), directionality (Steiger), and leave-one-out were assessed. A reverse MR (cognition→MCP) and two-step mediation MR (via depression, C-reactive protein, insomnia) were performed. The China CHARLS five-wave cohort (n=25,762 overall; 40,135 person-waves from the three cognition-assessed waves 2011/2013/2015, comprising 20,522 unique participants) longitudinal analysis of pain-site burden on cognitive decline is reported in §2.7 and §3.5.

**Results.** Genetically predicted MCP was associated with **lower** cognitive performance (IVW β=−0.361, 95% CI −0.424 to −0.299, p=3.65×10⁻³⁰; MR-Egger β=−0.362, intercept p=0.34; weighted-median β=−0.949; leave-one-out β range −0.386 to −0.315), with substantial heterogeneity (Cochran Q=242.5, I² 83.5%, p<10⁻³⁰) but no detectable directional pleiotropy (Egger intercept). The fixed-effect IVW estimate (−0.361) is the primary estimate per the pre-specified analysis plan (§2.4); the weighted-median estimate (−0.949) and the random-effects IVW estimate (−0.403) were both more extreme than the fixed-effect estimate, and the weighted median lay outside the fixed-effect IVW 95% CI (−0.424, −0.299); this reflects between-SNP inconsistency under high heterogeneity (I² 83.5%) rather than a robust corroboration of the fixed-effect estimate, so the true magnitude is uncertain and the result is hypothesis-generating; the Steiger directionality test was non-significant (p=0.23) but is invalid under UKB sample overlap, so it could not assess directionality (the test was performed forward-only). A non-UKB overlap-free re-framing of the same §3.1 FinnGen all-cause dementia and IGAP Alzheimer's disease estimates (neither shares UKB samples with the MCP exposure) pointed in the same clinical direction as the UKB cognitive result; the IGAP AD estimate was significant (IVW β=0.466, 95% CI 0.123–0.809, p=0.008) — this is the same estimate reported in §3.1, re-framed under an overlap-free label rather than an independent replication, and therefore provides no new evidence against UKB overlap — whereas all-cause dementia was non-significant (β=0.234, p=0.27) and Ben Nevis AD null (β=0.168, p=0.29). A formal overlap-bias quantification using the Burgess–Thompson (2016) estimator (the theoretical basis of the MRlap package, which could not be executed in-environment; §3.1.2) indicated that UKB sample overlap induces ≤3% bias on the MCP→cognition estimate under the assumed scenarios and does not alter its significance. Reverse MR (cognition→MCP) was also significant (IVW β=−0.171, p=8.85×10⁻⁵⁸), leaving causal direction uncertain. No mediator was significant (indirect effects p>0.11); the two-step mediation was underpowered, so these nulls do **not** establish a direct effect. In CHARLS (20,522 participants; 40,135 person-waves with complete cognition in 2011/2013/2015), each additional pain site was cross-sectionally associated with lower global cognition (β=−0.011 per site, p<0.001) with a significant nonlinear dose–response (P<0.001), but pain-site burden did not significantly accelerate cognitive change in the adjusted CHARLS longitudinal models (pain×time interaction p=0.70); the unadjusted m0 model — the primary three-wave longitudinal structure — showed a significant positive interaction (β=+0.0024, p<0.001), most plausibly a confounding/selection artefact rather than a protective effect (§3.5), and pain burden did not predict incident worst-quartile impairment (Cox HR 1.006 per site, 95% CI 0.988–1.024, p=0.53); the MR cognitive-performance endpoint is a cross-sectional score, not a decline measure. A four-wave sensitivity extension including the 2018 cognition module again found no significant incident-impairment hazard (HR 1.005, 95% CI 0.993–1.018, p=0.38); a nominally significant but *positive* pain×time interaction in that extension reflected the easier 2018 word list rather than true decline (§3.5.1). A change-score (ANCOVA) sensitivity additionally yielded a nominally significant but directionally inconsistent pain→decline association (β=−0.0067 per site, p=0.016) that we interpret cautiously because it diverges from the null LMM and Cox analyses (§3.5.2).

**Conclusions.** Genetic evidence is **compatible** with an association between genetically predicted MCP and lower cognitive performance, but the causal direction is uncertain (significant reverse MR; non-significant Steiger) and the cognitive-performance result, although UKB-sourced and thus subject to sample overlap, was little affected by overlap (≤3% of effect magnitude under the assumed scenarios; §3.1.2) and is directionally consistent with overlap-free dementia/AD analyses. The dementia/AD signal requires replication and is inconsistent across consortia. No mediator was identified; the mechanism remains unknown. The CHARLS longitudinal data corroborate the *direction* of the association (greater pain burden ↔ poorer cognition, dose-dependent) but provide only weak longitudinal evidence of accelerated decline over the observed window, consistent with a modest causal effect and the limited sensitivity of repeated cognitive testing in this cohort.

## 1. Introduction

Chronic pain affects a substantial proportion of middle-aged and older adults and is increasingly recognised as more than a sensory symptom — it is associated with accelerated cognitive ageing, incident dementia, and reduced reserve [1]. Multisite chronic pain (MCP), defined by pain at multiple body sites, carries a particularly high burden and has been linked cross-sectionally to poorer cognition [1]. The most definitive observational evidence to date comes from a UK Biobank prospective cohort of 356,383 dementia-free participants followed for a median of 13.3 years, in which each additional chronic pain site was associated with an 8% higher incidence of all-cause dementia (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.05–1.11) and a 9% higher incidence of Alzheimer's disease (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.13) in a dose–response manner [1]. Because that study, like most of this literature, is observational, its estimates remain vulnerable to residual confounding (notably by depression, sleep, and analgesic use) and to reverse causation, in which incipient neurodegeneration alters pain perception and reporting; the causal status of the pain–cognition association therefore remains unresolved [1].

Beyond the Tian et al. UKB cohort [1], a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies [2] synthesised the prospective evidence linking chronic pain to cognitive decline or dementia, and a CHARLS-based study [3] reported an association between multisite chronic pain and lower cognitive performance in a middle-aged and older Chinese population. A bidirectional MR framework has been applied to pain and related traits [4]; the present analysis extends this literature by (i) formalising the overlap-bias quantification (§3.1.2), (ii) reporting a non-UKB disease-endpoint re-framing (§3.1.1), and (iii) embedding the MR result in an independent Chinese longitudinal cohort (§3.5).

Two obstacles limit causal inference from observational cohorts. First, **reverse causation**: poorer cognition may increase pain reporting and reduce pain coping. Second, **confounding** by age, depression, sleep, and disability is profound and rarely fully adjusted. Mendelian randomization (MR) mitigates these by using genetic variants as instruments for pain, under the assumptions of relevance, exchangeability, and exclusion-restriction.

We therefore conducted a bidirectional two-sample MR to (i) estimate the effect of genetically predicted MCP on cognitive performance, Alzheimer's disease (AD), and all-cause dementia; (ii) test the reverse direction; (iii) probe mediators (depression, C-reactive protein [CRP], insomnia) via two-step MR. To embed the genetic findings in real-world epidemiology, we additionally analysed the Chinese CHARLS 2011–2020 cohort longitudinally for the association of pain-site burden with cognitive decline (now completed; see §2.7, §3.5). A key limitation, addressed transparently below, is that the headline cognitive-performance outcome and the MCP exposure are both UK Biobank–derived, creating sample overlap that constrains causal interpretation.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Study design
This study combines a two-sample MR core with population-based descriptive context, reported per the STROBE-MR 2021 guideline [6].

### 2.2 Data sources

**Exposure — multisite chronic pain (MCP).** Summary statistics from Johnston et al. (2019 [7]; UK Biobank, n=387,649, European ancestry) were obtained locally (DOI 10.5525/gla.researchdata.822). MCP was defined as pain at ≥2 (multisite) body sites.

**Outcomes (OpenGWAS / IEU).** Cognitive performance: `ebi-a-GCST006572` — a UK Biobank–derived cognitive-performance GWAS (1-standardised score; n=257,841; European ancestry). *Provenance note:* the GWAS Catalog / OpenGWAS metadata for this accession is listed under Lee et al. (Nat Genet 2018; PMID 30038396), which is an **educational-attainment** GWAS; the summary-statistic set itself is a UKB-derived cognitive-performance score and is cited here by trait and accession. Because both the MCP exposure and this outcome are UKB-sourced, **sample overlap is present** (see §2.4 and Limitations). *Endpoint caveat:* this accession is a **single-time-point, UKB-derived cognitive-performance score dominated by fluid reasoning (verbal–numeric reasoning)**, not a measure of longitudinal cognitive *decline*, and not a dementia or mild-cognitive-impairment endpoint; a 1-SD lower score should be read as lower measured cognitive performance at assessment and must not be equated with a diagnosis of cognitive impairment. Alzheimer's disease: IGAP 2013 [8] (`ieu-a-297`, n=54,162 [17,008 cases / 37,154 controls]). Ben Nevis/ADGC consortium [9] (`ieu-b-2`, n=63,926 [21,982 / 41,944]). All-cause dementia: FinnGen [10] (`finn-b-F5_DEMENTIA`, Freeze 5, n=216,771 [7,284 cases], 2021).

**Mediators.** Major depression (`ieu-b-102`, PGC, n=500,199 [11]); C-reactive protein (`ebi-a-GCST90029070`, Said 2022 [12], n=575,531); insomnia (`ukb-b-3957`, MRC-IEU, n=462,341 [13]).

**Descriptive context.** NHANES 2011–2018 (CDC) for descriptive correlates of subjective memory complaint (no pain variable; descriptive only). CHARLS 2011–2020 (Peking University [14]) longitudinal analysis of pain-site burden on cognitive decline is now completed and reported in §2.7 and §3.5.

### 2.3 Instrumental variable selection
MCP SNPs were selected at genome-wide significance (P<5×10⁻⁸) and LD-clumped (r²<0.001 within 10,000 kb; 1,000 Genomes European reference) using `ieugwasr`. Palindromic/ambiguous SNPs were dropped. This yielded **51 independent lead SNPs**, of which 41–43 were harmonised into the cognitive/dementia outcomes. The reverse (cognition→MCP) analysis **selected a separate set of 146 independent cognitive instruments** (not a subset of the 51 MCP SNPs). *These 146 SNPs derive from the same UKB-derived cognitive-performance GWAS used as the forward outcome, so the reverse direction is affected by UKB sample overlap at least as heavily as the forward direction* (see §3.2). Instrument strength was strong: across the 41–43 harmonised SNPs the mean per-SNP F-statistic was ≈33 (minimum ≈22), far above the conventional weak-instrument threshold of 10, indicating adequate relevance.

### 2.4 MR analysis
Primary estimate: inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) fixed-effect [15]. Sensitivity: MR-Egger (pleiotropy via intercept) [16], weighted median (robust to ≤50% invalid instruments) [17]. MR-Egger standard errors use the **fixed-effect (σ²=1) convention** (the TwoSampleMR default and the convention used for all reported Egger values; the dispersion-corrected alternative yields an intercept p≈0.70 and is noted only as a sensitivity; under I²=83.5% (residual σ̂²≈6.2) the dispersion-corrected SE is wider, so the fixed-effect convention is reported for consistency with TwoSampleMR and the deposited CSV rather than as a more conservative interval). Heterogeneity: Cochran Q, reported together with I² = (Q−df)/Q to convey the proportion of variation due to heterogeneity rather than sampling error [e.g., cognition I² 83.5%]. Directionality: a Steiger-type test [18] (custom implementation; approximates TwoSampleMR `directionality`; tests whether the exposure explains more variance than the outcome). Because both the MCP exposure and the cognitive-performance outcome are UKB-derived (overlapping), the Steiger test's independence assumption is violated and its variance is biased, so it is invalid for assessing directionality. It was performed forward-only and was non-significant (p=0.23), but this result can neither establish nor refute the dominant causal direction; directionality remains unresolved (Limitation 1). Leave-one-out: re-estimation excluding each SNP. All methods implemented in R (ieugwasr 1.1.0 [19]; TwoSampleMR unavailable in-environment).

**Effect-size units.** For the continuous cognitive-performance outcome, every β is reported as the change per **1-SD genetically predicted increase in MCP** (i.e., the SNP-instrumented MCP liability). For binary outcomes (AD, all-cause dementia) and the binary NHANES models, β is on the log-odds scale per 1-SD genetically predicted MCP increase. All CIs are 95%.

*Two distinct concepts are reported separately and must not be conflated:* (a) a **non-significant MR-Egger intercept** indicates *no detectable directional (balanced) pleiotropy*; (b) a **non-significant Steiger test** indicates that the genetic-correlation structure does *not* establish the exposure→outcome direction. They answer different questions.

*Outlier robustness.* The Statistical Analysis Plan (§4.3) specified MR-PRESSO and outlier-SNP re-estimation. These could **not** be executed because the TwoSampleMR / mr_presso stack was unavailable in the analysis environment (only `ieugwasr` ran). As the primary outlier-robustness proxy we rely on leave-one-out (no single SNP was influential; §3.1) and report this limitation explicitly. MR-PRESSO / Radial MR are recommended as future work once the full TwoSampleMR stack is available. In the interim, the IVW fixed-effect vs random-effects gap (−0.361 vs −0.403) and the weighted-median divergence (−0.949, more extreme than IVW and outside its 95% CI) are reported as a partial substitute for MR-PRESSO's pleiotropy detection.

*Statistical framework.* The primary hypothesis was MCP→cognitive performance; all other directions and the three mediation paths were **exploratory**. No formal multiple-comparison correction was applied, but all p-values are reported to allow reader judgement. Across the 7 exploratory directions (3 dementia/AD outcomes, reverse MR, 3 mediation paths) no multiple-comparison correction was applied; the pre-specified primary (MCP→cognitive performance) remains significant after Bonferroni/BH, whereas the exploratory IGAP AD signal (p=0.008) survives BH but not Bonferroni.

### 2.4.1 Overlap-bias quantification (Burgess–Thompson framework; the theoretical basis of MRlap)
Both the MCP exposure (Johnston et al. 2019 [7]; UK Biobank, n=387,649) and the cognitive-performance outcome (`ebi-a-GCST006572`; UKB-derived, n=257,841) are UKB-sourced, so sample overlap is present (Limitation 4). We quantified its impact with the overlap-bias estimator of Burgess, Davies & Thompson (2016) [20] — the same theoretical basis as the MRlap package (Mounier & Kutalik 2021) [21], which additionally corrects winner's curse and weak-instrument bias. The estimator is `bias = ols_bias × ρ × (1/F)`, where ρ is the overlap proportion, F the instrument F-statistic, and `ols_bias` the (unknown) observational confounding bias; the relative bias equals 1/F. We used the mean per-SNP F-statistic across the 41 harmonised SNPs (F≈33, matching §2.3) and the literature-derived SNP heritabilities for context (MCP h²=0.102 [7]; cognitive performance h²≈0.25 [22]). Because the canonical MRlap R package [21] could **not** be executed in the analysis environment (no compiler toolchain for its GenomicSEM/OpenMx dependencies; LD-score reference files were unreachable), the analytic Burgess–Thompson estimator was used as a transparent sensitivity analysis; its formula was verified against the authors' own implementation (mglev1n/mrSampleOverlap).

### 2.5 Two-step mediation MR
Path MCP → mediator → cognition, using the two-step MR mediation framework [23]. Indirect effect = β₁(MCP→mediator) × β₂(mediator→cognition); 95% CI by the delta method. Mediation results are interpreted as **suggestive only** [23] and were underpowered (see §3.3).

### 2.6 NHANES auxiliary analysis (moved to Supplementary Note 1)
The NHANES 2011–2018 descriptive analysis of subjective memory complaint (no pain variable; descriptive only) is reported in Supplementary Note 1, because the analysed cycles contain no chronic-pain variable overlapping with the cognitive-assessment module in the same respondents and therefore do not test the pain→cognition hypothesis.

### 2.7 CHARLS longitudinal analysis
The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged ≥45 years [14]. We analysed all five waves (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, 2020) to provide population-based, longitudinal context for the MR association. Every variable was empirically verified against the source `.dta` files; the full per-wave mapping is documented in `data/raw/charls/CHARLS_VARIABLE_MAP.md`.

**Exposure — pain-site burden.** For the three primary cognition-assessed waves (2011, 2013, 2015) the pain-site count was derived as the number of non-missing entries among `da042s1`–`da042s15`. These variables store the body-site index when a site is painful and are missing when that site is painless, so the count of non-missing entries equals the number of painful sites (range 0–15). A binary `pain_any` flag was derived per wave (2011/2015: `da041`==1; 2013: 5-level intensity `wb16`≥2). Because the 2018 and 2020 waves used a different cognition instrument and a minimal demographic file, they were retained only for descriptive pain prevalence; their cognition was set to missing by design and excluded from trajectory modelling.

**Outcome — memory-and-attention composite (hereafter "global cognition").** For 2011/2013/2015 we computed three exactly-scored components: (i) immediate word recall (`dc006s1`–`dc006s11` in 2011; best-of-three learning trials `dc006_{1,2,3}_s1`–`dc006_{1,2,3}_s11` in 2013; `dc006s1`–`dc006s12` in 2015), (ii) delayed word recall (`dc027s*`; 11/11/12 words), and (iii) serial-7 subtraction (`dc019`–`dc023`, scored 0–5 against the fixed sequence 93/86/79/72/65). Orientation and figure-drawing items were deliberately excluded from the composite because their scoring requires per-respondent interview dates that are not available in the public files. Each component was **cross-sample** z-standardised (across all primary-wave person-observations, not within-wave) so that the baseline (2011) quartile cut and the longitudinal trajectories are on a common scale; the 11- versus 12-word list difference in 2015 is absorbed by this z-scoring. The global cognition z-score is the mean of the three component z-scores.

**Analysis.** The primary analytic sample comprised person-waves from 2011/2013/2015 with complete cognition (40,135 person-waves; 20,522 unique participants). After sequential covariate adjustment the fully adjusted models analysed 15,256 person-waves (14,913 unique participants), a 62% attrition from covariate missingness disclosed here and in §3.5. Linear mixed models (LMM) estimated `cog_global_z ~ pain_burden × time + covariates + (1|id)`, where `time = wave − 2011` (0/2/4) and a random intercept accounted for repeated measures; covariates were added sequentially (m0: none; m1: age, sex, education-years, urban, married; m2: + smoking, alcohol, sleep hours, comorbidity count). A Cox proportional-hazards model estimated the hazard of *incident cognitive impairment*, defined as a participant non-impaired at the 2011 baseline (above the worst quartile of baseline `cog_global_z`) who later fell into the worst quartile at a subsequent wave; person-time began at the 2011 baseline and ended at the wave of first impairment or last follow-up. A restricted cubic spline (RCS, 3 knots) modelled the baseline dose–response of `cog_global_z` on `pain_burden`. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of ≥2 of five self-reported CHARLS chronic conditions (diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, cancer), i.e. comorb_count ≥ 2, and is reported as % multimorbidity in the CHARLS baseline table (Panel A).

**Sensitivity — four-wave extension (2011–2018).** To test robustness to the 2018 cognition assessment, we additionally encoded the CHARLS 2018 "DC Cognition" module (user-provided `Cognition.zip`; 212 `dc*_w4` variables) into the longitudinal panel. The 2018 immediate and delayed recall items are item-level and position-coded (`dc028_w4_sK`, `dc029_w4_sK`, `dc030_w4_sK` for immediate; `dc047_w4_sK` for delayed; K = 1…12, scoring 1 if the K-th word was correctly recalled and 0 otherwise — a best-of-three repetition design), which is distinct from the 2011/2013/2015 `dc006*`/`dc027*` scoring; serial-7 subtraction in 2018 is a single field (`dc014_w4_1_1`) and is not comparable to the 2013/2015 five-item sum, so it was excluded from the 2018 composite. We recomputed the cross-sample z-score on the four-wave sample using only immediate and delayed recall (`cog_global_z4 = mean(z(im), z(de))`), which absorbs the 2018 easier 12-word list as a level shift. The four-wave LMM and Cox models were then re-estimated over 2011/2013/2015/2018 (`time = wave − 2011`: 0/2/4/7). A further change-score (ANCOVA) sensitivity on the comparable three-wave sample is reported in §3.5.2.

### 2.8 Ethics
MR uses only published summary statistics; NHANES and CHARLS are public, de-identified, consent-covered datasets. This analysis is exempt from individual consent.

**Figure 1** (forest plot), **Figure 2** (SNP scatter with MR and MR-Egger fit lines), **Figure 3** (leave-one-out), and **Figure 4** (Wald-ratio funnel) illustrate the cognition result. Genetically predicted MCP was robustly associated with **lower** cognitive performance across IVW, Egger, and weighted median. The MR-Egger intercept was non-significant (p=0.34), indicating no detectable directional pleiotropy; leave-one-out estimates were stable (range −0.386 to −0.315). **Heterogeneity was high** (Cochran Q=242.5, I² 83.5%): the random-effects IVW estimate (β=−0.403, SE 0.075) is even more extreme than the fixed-effect point estimate, and the weighted-median estimate (−0.949, SE 0.044) was more extreme in magnitude than the fixed-effect IVW point estimate (−0.361) and lay outside its 95% CI (−0.424, −0.299). This pattern — a weighted median *more* extreme than IVW under high heterogeneity — is inconsistent with balanced horizontal pleiotropy (which would attenuate, not amplify, the weighted-median estimate) and instead reflects substantial between-SNP inconsistency. Because the >50%-valid-instrument assumption underlying the weighted median is untestable at I² 83.5%, the weighted median is a sensitivity analysis rather than a primary estimate; the fixed-effect IVW estimate (−0.361) remains the primary point estimate per the pre-specified §2.4, and the true causal magnitude is uncertain given the marked estimator divergence (IVW −0.361, weighted median −0.949, random-effects −0.403), so the forward cognition result is hypothesis-generating. For the lower-power disease outcomes, the weighted-median estimates were also unstable (1.015, −0.703, −0.261 for all-cause dementia, IGAP-AD, Ben Nevis-AD; Table 2) and are reported as sensitivity values only. The **Steiger directionality test was non-significant (p=0.23)** but is invalid under UKB overlap, so it could not establish (nor refute) MCP→cognition as the dominant causal direction. The dementia/AD signal was **inconsistent**: all-cause dementia null, IGAP AD positive, Ben Nevis AD null.

### 3.1.1 Re-framing of the §3.1 FinnGen and IGAP estimates under an overlap-free label (no new analysis)

The primary cognitive-performance result is constrained by UKB sample overlap with the MCP exposure (§2.2, Limitation 4). The two non-UKB disease outcomes — all-cause dementia (FinnGen, a Finnish national biobank; `finn-b-F5_DEMENTIA`) and Alzheimer's disease (IGAP 2013 consortium [8]; `ieu-a-297`, n=54,162) — were already reported in §3.1; because neither shares UKB samples with the MCP exposure, they provide an overlap-free *re-framing* of the same estimates (Figure 5). Note that a non-UKB *cognitive-function* GWAS was not substituted as the headline outcome (only underpowered `ieu-a-16`, n=12,441, was available); the dementia/AD re-framing therefore provides overlap-robustness for the MCP→cognitive-*outcome* hypothesis only by extrapolation, not as a direct non-UKB cognition test. *These estimates are numerically identical to those in §3.1 (All-cause dementia and AD — IGAP); no new instruments, harmonization, or data were introduced — only a different framing that removes the UKB overlap concern.* This is therefore a *re-framing* of the same §3.1 estimates under an overlap-free label, not an independent replication; it provides no new statistical evidence about UKB overlap and must not be read as corroborating the cognition finding. Neither outcome shares UKB samples with the MCP exposure (FinnGen is Finnish; IGAP is an international case–control consortium), so direct sample overlap is largely absent — a cleaner test of the overlap concern than the UKB cognitive-performance outcome.

Both non-UKB outcomes point in the same clinical direction as the UKB cognitive-performance result (higher IGAP AD odds; lower UKB cognitive performance), but on scale-incomparable endpoints — a qualitative consistency of direction, not a quantitative corroboration: the AD effect is *positive* whereas the cognition effect is *negative*, which is not a coherent dose–response signal, and the AD finding is inconsistent across consortia (Ben Nevis and all-cause dementia null). All-cause dementia was non-significant (β=0.234, p=0.27), most plausibly reflecting the lower power of a binary trait with a smaller effective case sample rather than a true null. Taken together, the non-UKB re-framing points in the same clinical direction — higher IGAP AD odds and lower UKB cognitive performance — but the two estimates are on different scales (log-odds for a binary disease endpoint vs. SD for a continuous performance score), so their opposite signs cannot be combined into a single dose–response signal; the IGAP AD point estimate is significant but Ben Nevis AD and all-cause dementia are null, so the qualitative-direction concordance is fragile, scale-incomparable, and does not constitute independent corroboration of the UKB cognition result (the same §3.1 FinnGen/IGAP estimates, here re-framed under an overlap-free label); it provides a sensitivity re-framing, not independent corroboration, and does not strengthen inference about the cognition score itself, whereas the UKB result itself remains formally hypothesis-generating because its overlap cannot be excluded from summary statistics alone. ### 3.1.2 Overlap-bias quantification (Burgess–Thompson framework; the theoretical basis of MRlap)
To directly quantify the UKB sample-overlap concern on the MCP→cognition estimate, we applied the Burgess–Thompson (2016) [20] overlap-bias estimator (the same basis as the MRlap package [21]; §2.4.1), driven by the empirically computed instrument F-statistic (F≈33 from the 41 harmonised SNPs) and literature-derived SNP heritabilities (MCP h²=0.102 [7]; cognition h²≈0.25 [22]). The relative bias equals 1/F ≈ 3%. Across a grid of overlap proportions (ρ=0.3–1.0) and plausible observational-bias assumptions (|ols_bias| = 25–100% of the observed effect), the estimated overlap-induced bias on the MCP→cognition IVW estimate was **≤3.0% of the effect size** under the assumed same-direction observational scenarios (Table 4; the maximal grid point is 3.004%). The de-overlapped ("true") effect remained strongly negative (≈−0.35 to −0.36) and highly significant (p<0.001) at every grid point; under the causal null the overlap-attributable type-1 error rate was modest (5.0%–6.3% even at full overlap). Because two-sample overlap bias is typically directed toward the observational (confounded) association, the observed estimate is *plausibly* a conservative bound; however, the Burgess–Thompson estimator bounds bias *magnitude*, not direction, so a directionally opposite bias cannot be formally excluded. The sensitivity grid enumerates same-direction observational bias only; the sign-opposite scenario is listed as the bottom row of Table 4 (|ols_bias| = 100% opposite sign, Δβ +0.0109, 3.00% of effect, de-overlapped β −0.372), and the ≤3% *magnitude* bound holds for either sign of ols_bias provided |ols_bias|≤100% of the effect and ρ≤1, so |bias|≤1/F≈3%; this is a magnitude bound, however, and the true maximal grid point is 3.004%. Clinically, the ≤3% *magnitude* bound holds for either bias direction, but the Burgess–Thompson estimator does not establish the bias *direction*; the overlap-induced bias (≤3%) is far smaller than the effect size (−0.361) in magnitude, so the qualitative conclusion — lower genetically predicted cognitive performance with higher MCP liability — is robust to overlap in magnitude, although a directionally opposite bias cannot be formally excluded (see Conclusion).

**Conclusion.** The Burgess–Thompson estimator bounds the overlap-induced bias at ≤3% of the effect *magnitude* under the assumed (same-direction) observational scenarios (true maximum 3.004%), but it does not establish the bias *direction*; the observed negative association is therefore quantitatively little-affected by overlap under these assumptions, although a directionally opposite bias cannot be formally excluded. The association remains hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive, and is directionally consistent with (not independently corroborated by) the overlap-free dementia/AD re-framing in §3.1.1.

### 3.2 Reverse MR — cognition → MCP
IVW β=**−0.171** (95% CI −0.192 to −0.150, p=8.85×10⁻⁵⁸; Egger β=−0.172, intercept p=0.14; weighted median −0.121; Cochran Q=474.8, p=1.1×10⁻³⁶). **Both directions were significant.** However, the reverse direction (cognition→MCP) uses the same UKB-derived cognitive-performance GWAS as its exposure and the UKB MCP GWAS as its outcome, so it is affected by UKB sample overlap at least as heavily as the forward direction. The bidirectional significance therefore **cannot by itself** establish reciprocal causal effects or shared genetic architecture — it may partly reflect the common UKB overlap affecting both directions; a non-overlapping reverse analysis is still lacking. A clean one-way causal arrow cannot be asserted on the basis of bidirectional significance alone. As with the forward direction, this reverse Egger intercept is itself computed under UKB overlap, so its non-significance speaks only to directional pleiotropy, not to overlap. The reverse-direction weighted-median estimate (−0.121) was generated by the same `wmedian()` function as the forward direction; because the reverse harmonized dataset was not cached in-environment for this round, this value should be re-confirmed when the full pipeline is re-executed (the same 50%-cumulative-weight boundary sensitivity applies).

### 3.5 CHARLS longitudinal — pain-site burden and cognition
The primary analytic sample included 20,522 participants (40,135 person-waves across 2011/2013/2015 with complete cognition; after sequential covariate adjustment the fully adjusted models analysed 15,256 person-waves (14,913 unique participants), a 62% attrition from covariate missingness, §2.7). Because the adjusted models retain only ≈1.0–1.04 person-waves per participant (m2: 15,256/14,913; m1: 15,618/15,034), m1 and m2 are quasi-cross-sectional, and the longitudinal LMM inference therefore rests mainly on the unadjusted m0 structure (40,135 person-waves / 20,522 unique); the cross-sectional m2 effect remains the fully adjusted estimate reported below. Table 6 summarises the 2011 baseline by pain-burden category and the longitudinal models. In the fully adjusted LMM (m2), each additional pain site was cross-sectionally associated with lower global cognition (β = −0.0112 per site, SE 0.0027, p<0.001), and this association was robust across progressively adjusted models (m0 β=−0.0257; m1 β=−0.0125; all p<0.001). A restricted cubic spline confirmed a highly significant nonlinear dose–response (overall P<0.001; nonlinear P<0.001): predicted global cognition z fell from −0.020 (0 sites) to −0.077 (1 site), −0.114 (2 sites), and −0.133 (3 sites; the ≥3 subgroup mean is reported in Table 6 Panel A for descriptive context), with the steepest decrement at low pain burden (Figure 7).

Longitudinally, pain-site burden did **not** significantly accelerate cognitive change over 2011–2015 in the adjusted models (m1 β=+0.0008, p=0.68; m2 β=+0.0008, p=0.70); however, the unadjusted m0 model — the primary three-wave longitudinal structure (40,135 person-waves / 20,522 unique participants) — showed a **significant, positive** `pain_burden × time` interaction (β=+0.0024, SE 0.0007, p<0.001), i.e., higher pain burden tracked with *less* cognitive decline (or more improvement) over time, the opposite of a decline-acceleration and, absent covariate adjustment (CESD-depression was not included; Limitation 6), most plausibly a confounding/selection artefact rather than a protective pain effect; the adjusted models show no significant decline-acceleration. Consistent with the null adjusted picture, in the Cox model the hazard of incident worst-quartile cognitive impairment per additional pain site was 1.006 (95% CI 0.988–1.024, p=0.53; 10,087 participants at risk, 1,624 events), not statistically significant, although the point estimate was directionally consistent with harm.

In summary, CHARLS provides population-based corroboration of the MR *direction* (greater pain burden ↔ poorer cognition, dose-dependent) in an independent, middle-aged/older Chinese population. The longitudinal decline-acceleration signal was non-significant in the adjusted three-wave LMM models (m1/m2) and in the Cox analysis; the unadjusted m0 LMM interaction was significant and positive (the opposite of decline; Limitation 6), and the four-wave extension's nominally significant *positive* LMM interaction reflects the easier 2018 word list rather than true decline (see §3.5.1 / Limitations #6). A change-score (ANCOVA) sensitivity additionally produced a divergent, significant pain→decline association that we interpret cautiously (§3.5.2). This pattern is compatible with a modest true effect and with the constraints of self-reported pain and repeated cognitive testing (see Limitations). The cross-sectional per-site effect (−0.011 SD; ≈0.11 SD across 0→≥3 sites) and the null Cox hazard (HR 1.006 per site, 95% CI 0.988–1.024) are modest in magnitude and should not be read as clinically decisive. For clinical context, the cross-sectional per-site effect of −0.011 SD corresponds to roughly 0.03 MMSE points per pain site (assuming a population SD of ≈3 points for the MMSE, a rough anchoring only). No MID has been established for the present composite endpoint; by way of context, the MMSE and MoCA instruments themselves have approximate MIDs of 1–3 points and 2.2 points respectively, but these are not directly applicable to the present cross-sample memory-and-attention composite (Limitation 10). The Cox hazard of 1.006 per pain site (95% CI 0.988–1.024) is a null clinical result. Because CESD-depression was not included as a covariate in these models (Limitation 6), the cross-sectional *magnitude* — not only the direction — may be partly confounded by depression and should be read as hypothesis-generating rather than an independent confirmation. The CHARLS signal is therefore consistent with a subclinical population-level association that is not individually detectable, and the per-site magnitude is far below any established MID for cognitive endpoints.

### 3.5.1 Four-wave sensitivity analysis (2011–2018)

We repeated the trajectory and Cox models after additionally encoding the 2018 "DC Cognition" module. The four-wave analytic sample comprised 72,693 person-waves (across 2011/2013/2015/2018; 16,250 at risk, 3,064 events in the Cox model). The Cox hazard of incident worst-quartile impairment per additional pain site was **1.005 (95% CI 0.993–1.018, p=0.381)** — non-significant and materially unchanged from the three-wave estimate (HR 1.006, p=0.53), confirming that no decline-acceleration signal emerges over the extended seven-year window. However, the four-wave LMM `pain_burden × time` interaction became **nominally significant but positive** (m2 β=+0.00153, SE 0.00061, t=2.52, p=0.012), the opposite direction to a true decline-acceleration.

This is a **measurement artefact**. The 2018 wave introduces a 12-word recall list that is markedly easier than the 11-word lists of 2011/2013/2015 and uses a different (position-coded, best-of-three) response format; consequently the 2018 cohort-level cognition jumps to a cross-sample z of +0.919 (versus −0.33, −0.22, and −0.22 in 2011, 2013, and 2015 respectively). Because the cross-sample z-score absorbs this level shift, the `time` slope — and any `pain_burden × time` product built on it — is dominated by the 2018 instrument change rather than genuine cognitive change. We therefore do **not** interpret the positive interaction as a pain effect, and report it transparently as a sensitivity caveat (see Limitations #6). The positive pain×time interaction is also clinically counter-intuitive — pain should accelerate decline rather than accelerate improvement — which itself supports the measurement-artefact interpretation. A formal psychometric equating of the 2018 word list against the 2011/2013/2015 lists (e.g., via item-response theory or published Chinese older-adult word-frequency norms) would strengthen this attribution; we disclose the artefact transparently but cannot formally equate the lists with available data. The null incident-impairment hazard is unaffected because the Cox event definition is anchored to the *baseline (2011)* worst-quartile and tracks subsequent falls, which are not contaminated by the 2018 level shift in the same way the continuous LMM slope is.

### 3.5.2 Change-score (ANCOVA) sensitivity analysis

To triangulate the longitudinal pain–cognition association with a methodologically distinct approach, we additionally modelled cognitive change with a change-score (analysis-of-covariance) design on the comparable three-wave sample (2011/2013/2015); the 2018 wave was excluded because its cognition instrument is not comparable (§3.5.1). For each participant with cognition at baseline (2011) and a follow-up wave we computed the raw change Δcog = cog_global_z(follow-up) − cog_global_z(2011), using the same cross-sample z global cognition score as the primary LMM, and regressed it on baseline pain-site burden under three specifications: (M1) unadjusted; (M2) covariate-adjusted (age, sex, education-years, urban, married, smoking, alcohol, sleep hours, comorbidity count); and (M3) a full ANCOVA further adjusting for baseline cognition. Robust (HC1) standard errors were used.

In the 2011→2015 window (n=8,493 in the adjusted models), the unadjusted and covariate-adjusted models showed no significant pain–change association (M1 β=+0.0054, p=0.11; M2 β=+0.0024, p=0.53). However, the full ANCOVA that additionally adjusted for baseline cognition yielded a nominally significant *negative* association: each additional pain site was associated with a larger 2011→2015 decline of β=−0.0067 (SE 0.0028, p=0.016); the 2011→2013 window was consistent (β=−0.0063, p=0.027; Table 6, Panel E).

This change-score signal **diverges** from the primary LMM pain×time interaction (β=+0.0008, p=0.70) and from the incident-impairment Cox model (HR 1.006, p=0.53), and we do **not** interpret it as evidence of accelerated decline. The divergence is the expected signature of baseline imbalance combined with regression to the mean: pain-site burden is itself strongly associated with *lower* baseline cognition (β=−0.011 per site, p<0.001), so conditioning the change score on baseline cognition (Lord's paradox) inverts and amplifies the apparent pain effect on change. The LMM, which models the trajectory jointly without partitioning variance into a baseline-conditioned change score, and the Cox model, which anchors events to the baseline worst-quartile and tracks subsequent falls, are less susceptible to this artefact and are our preferred inference for decline. We therefore report the change-score result transparently as a sensitivity caveat: it does not overturn the primary conclusion that, over the observed window, no robust signal of pain-accelerated cognitive decline was detected. (Reproducible from `data/derived/charls_results_changescore_20260921.rds` and `charls_changescore_table_20260921.csv`; script `analysis/01c_charls_changescore.R`.)

## 4. Discussion

Our MR core is **compatible** with an association between genetically predicted multisite chronic pain and **lower cognitive performance**, robust to Egger and weighted-median sensitivity analyses, with no detectable directional pleiotropy. However, the significant **reverse** direction and the **non-significant Steiger directionality test (p=0.23)** — which is invalid under UKB overlap and therefore cannot assess directionality — together leave the causal direction unresolved — we therefore describe the finding as an association rather than assert a unilateral causal arrow. The weighted-median estimate (−0.949) was more extreme than IVW and lay outside the fixed-effect IVW 95% CI (−0.424, −0.299), and together with high heterogeneity (I² 83.5%) signals between-SNP inconsistency and possible **horizontal (non-directional) pleiotropy**, which the non-significant Egger intercept does not rule out (see Limitations). The AD/dementia signal was inconsistent across consortia — IGAP positive, Ben Nevis and all-cause dementia null — and should be read as hypothesis-generating. Framed as a **non-UKB overlap-free re-framing** (§3.1.1; the same §3.1 FinnGen dementia and IGAP AD estimates, re-framed under an overlap-free label), the IGAP AD estimate (significant, overlap-free) and the Finnish all-cause dementia estimate point in the same clinical direction as the UKB cognition result on scale-incomparable endpoints; this is a re-framing of the same estimates, however, and therefore provides no independent statistical evidence against UKB overlap — it is a sensitivity framing, not cross-source corroboration. The non-significant all-cause dementia result most plausibly reflects the limited power of a binary trait with a smaller effective case sample.

**Comparison with prior evidence.** Our genetically predicted MCP→lower-cognitive-performance signal is broadly consistent with the only other comprehensive pain–cognition MR published to date: Guo et al. (2023) [24], using a bidirectional MR framework across nine chronic-pain phenotypes and multiple cognitive and dementia endpoints, reported that multisite chronic pain was associated with worse cognitive function (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.53–0.89; p=0.043) but found no significant reverse effect of cognitive status on pain and no significant analgesic–cognition associations. Our study extends that work by (i) quantifying UKB sample-overlap bias explicitly with the Burgess–Thompson estimator (§3.1.2) rather than leaving it unaddressed, (ii) reporting a non-UKB disease-endpoint re-framing (§3.1.1), and (iii) embedding the genetic signal in an independent Chinese longitudinal cohort (CHARLS, §3.5). The observational cohort literature, by contrast, remains inconsistent: although large UKB-derived prospective studies report a dose–response pain→dementia association [1], a 24-year PAQUID cohort found chronic pain *not* significantly linked to long-term dementia risk after multivariate adjustment (aHR 1.23, 95% CI 0.88–1.73; p=0.23) [25], and a recent UKB-based longitudinal study of chronic widespread pain reported observational hazard ratios of 1.53 for dementia and 2.55 for mild cognitive impairment yet a non-significant Mendelian randomization estimate (β=1.50, p_adj=0.076) [26] — a divergence between observational and genetic evidence that mirrors the present study's pattern of an observationally/CHARLS directionally consistent signal alongside a non-significant or overlap-constrained genetic dementia endpoint. This mixed landscape argues against over-reading any single null or positive result and supports our framing of the dementia/AD findings as hypothesis-generating.

Mechanistically, no tested mediator (depression, CRP, insomnia) was significant. Because the two-step mediation was underpowered, this does **not** establish a direct pathway; central sensitisation, neuroinflammation, or HPA-axis dysregulation remain **speculative** possibilities requiring experimental validation. Clinically, the significant reverse direction (cognition→MCP) is plausible via two non-exclusive pathways: (i) early neurodegenerative changes (white-matter hyperintensities, grey-matter atrophy) that lower cognitive performance also disrupt descending pain modulation, increasing multisite pain reporting; or (ii) shared genetic architecture in neurotransmitter pathways (e.g., serotonin, BDNF) that jointly influence cognition and pain perception. The MR design cannot distinguish these, and the bidirectional significance is best read as evidence of a shared genetic substrate rather than a unilateral causal arrow from cognition to pain.

The dementia/AD effects point in the same clinical direction as the cognition result but on scale-incomparable endpoints (higher AD odds vs lower cognitive performance), and the *positive* AD sign relative to the *negative* cognition sign is not a coherent dose–response signal, and the AD result is inconsistent across consortia (Ben Nevis and all-cause dementia null) and may reflect different AD GWAS definitions or insufficient power in the binary dementia outcomes; it should not be read as corroborating the cognition finding, and the sign contrast is acknowledged rather than resolved. The limited clinical translation of AD genetic signals is further underscored by the marginal efficacy of amyloid-targeting monoclonal antibodies [5].

**Interpretation of magnitude.** The headline estimate should not be read as a large clinical effect. It is expressed per 1-SD of genetically predicted MCP liability, and the endpoint is a single-occasion performance score rather than a decline rate or a diagnosis (§2.2); the CHARLS per-site estimate (−0.011 SD) similarly implies that three additional painful sites correspond to ≈0.11 SD of global cognition (0 to ≥3 sites, per the §3.5 RCS). The CHARLS Cox hazard of 1.006 per pain site (95% CI 0.988–1.024, p=0.53) for incident worst-quartile cognitive impairment is numerically much smaller and statistically null compared with the per-site HR of 1.08 for incident all-cause dementia reported by Tian et al. (2023) in the UK Biobank [1]. The discrepancy most plausibly reflects (i) the different endpoint (worst-quartile cognitive performance vs. clinically diagnosed all-cause dementia), (ii) the much shorter CHARLS follow-up window (4 years vs. 13.3 years median in UKB), (iii) the older Chinese cohort's different baseline dementia risk and comorbidity structure, and (iv) the cross-sample z-score's sensitivity to practice effects in this short window. The CHARLS null result should not be over-read as refutation of the UKB finding, but nor should it be presented as consistent with it.

### CHARLS longitudinal context

The CHARLS component adds population-based, longitudinal context to the MR association. Consistent with the MR direction, greater pain-site burden was robustly associated with lower global cognition cross-sectionally (per-site β=−0.011, p<0.001 in the fully adjusted model) and showed a significant nonlinear dose–response: predicted global cognition z fell from −0.020 (0 sites) to −0.077 (1 site), −0.114 (2 sites), and −0.133 (3 sites; the ≥3 subgroup mean is in Table 6 Panel A; Figure 7). However, the longitudinal decline-acceleration signal was weak. Across the three cognition-assessed waves (2011/2013/2015), pain-site burden did not significantly accelerate cognitive change in the adjusted LMM models (m1/m2 β=+0.0008, p=0.68–0.70); in the unadjusted m0 model the interaction was significant and positive (β=+0.0024, p<0.001) — higher pain burden tracked with *less* decline, the opposite of a decline-acceleration effect and most plausibly a confounding/selection artefact given the absence of covariate adjustment (Limitation 6) — and it did not significantly predict incident worst-quartile impairment in the Cox analysis (HR 1.006 per site, 95% CI 0.988–1.024, p=0.53). The point estimates were directionally consistent with harm, but the four-year window and the dominant practice effects of repeated cognitive testing limit power to detect a true decline-acceleration; self-reported pain and the non-comparable 2018/2020 cognition instruments further constrain sensitivity. A four-wave sensitivity extension that re-encoded the 2018 DC Cognition module (position-coded recall) and added the 2018 wave again found no significant incident-impairment hazard (Cox HR 1.005, p=0.381); it produced a nominally significant *positive* pain×time LMM interaction (β=+0.0015, p=0.012) that is driven by the easier 2018 word list (a level shift rather than true decline), which we disclose transparently rather than interpret as a pain effect (§3.5.1). We therefore read CHARLS as corroborating the *direction* of the MR association in an independent, middle-aged/older Chinese population, while the longitudinal trajectory evidence remains inconclusive — a pattern compatible with a modest causal effect; a change-score sensitivity additionally yielded a divergent but cautiously interpreted signal (§3.5.2).

### Limitations
1. **Reverse causation unresolved** — bidirectional significance. The reverse MR (cognition→MCP, §3.2) was also significant, but because both its exposure and outcome are UKB-sourced, it is subject to the same (indeed heavier) UKB sample-overlap bias as the forward direction. The bidirectional significance therefore cannot independently establish causal direction, reciprocal effects, or shared genetic architecture; it may partly reflect the common UKB overlap. A non-overlapping reverse analysis is still required. The Steiger test was performed in the forward direction only and is invalid under UKB sample overlap, so it cannot assess directionality; reverse-direction causality was therefore also not established by Steiger.
2. **Heterogeneity and possible horizontal pleiotropy.** The cognition IVW showed substantial heterogeneity (Cochran Q p<10⁻³⁰; I² 83.5%), indicating variant-level effect variation and arguing against a single homogeneous causal effect. The weighted-median estimate (−0.949, SE 0.044) is more extreme in magnitude than the fixed-effect IVW estimate (−0.361) and lies outside its 95% CI (−0.424, −0.299); this divergence — a weighted median *more* extreme than IVW under high heterogeneity — is inconsistent with balanced horizontal pleiotropy (which would attenuate the weighted median toward the null) and instead reflects between-SNP inconsistency, while non-directional pleiotropy remains possible because the non-significant Egger intercept (p=0.34) excludes only *directional* pleiotropy. The Statistical Analysis Plan specified MR-PRESSO / outlier-SNP re-estimation to clarify this, but those analyses could not be run (TwoSampleMR unavailable; Limitation 7); Radial MR is recommended as future work. Estimates remained directionally stable in leave-one-out. The weighted-median validity requires >50% of SNPs to be valid; with I² 83.5% this assumption is untestable from these data and may be violated, so the weighted median is a sensitivity estimate rather than assumption-free. The weighted-median value is also sensitive to the exact 50%-cumulative-weight threshold: the first SNP to cross 50% cumulative weight has Wald ratio −0.949 (cumulative 0.510), whereas the last SNP below the threshold has Wald ratio −0.233 (cumulative 0.486); a previously reported −0.233 arose from selecting the below-threshold SNP rather than the first at/above 50%, and the corrected value is −0.949. Its precise value is therefore indicative rather than exact.
3. **AD/dementia inconsistency** across GWAS sources limits inference on dementia specifically.
4. **Sample overlap (UKB×UKB).** The MCP exposure (Johnston 2019, UKB, n≈387,649) and the cognitive-performance outcome (`ebi-a-GCST006572`, UKB-derived, n=257,841) are **both UKB-sourced**, so direct sample overlap is present and cannot be excluded. Its magnitude cannot be precisely quantified from summary statistics alone, and the previous draft's claim that a "non-UKB COGENT cognition GWAS mitigates" this overlap is **incorrect** — `ebi-a-GCST006572` is itself UKB-derived. The MCP→cognitive-performance result must therefore be interpreted as **hypothesis-generating**. We did not substitute a non-UKB *cognitive-function* GWAS as the headline: the only readily available non-overlapping cognitive GWAS (`ieu-a-16`, childhood intelligence, n=12,441) is underpowered, and the larger cognitive GWAS (Davies 2018, n=300,486; Savage 2018, n=269,867) themselves incorporate UKB and would not remove the overlap. Instead, we conducted a **non-UKB overlap-free re-framing** (§3.1.1; the same FinnGen dementia and IGAP AD estimates as §3.1, re-framed under an overlap-free label) — both point in the same clinical direction as the UKB cognition result on scale-incomparable endpoints, with the IGAP AD estimate significant (β=0.466, p=0.008). Because these are the same §3.1 estimates re-framed, they do *not* corroborate the association independently of UKB overlap; they speak to dementia/AD *risk* rather than the cognitive-performance *score* itself, and all-cause dementia remained non-significant. Overlap-robust methods (MR with sample-overlap correction / MRlap) were applied (§2.4.1, §3.1.2): the canonical MRlap R package [21] could not be executed in-environment (no compiler toolchain for its GenomicSEM/OpenMx dependencies; LD-score reference files unreachable), so we used the analytic Burgess–Thompson (2016) [20] overlap-bias estimator — the same theoretical basis as MRlap — with literature-derived SNP heritabilities (MCP h²=0.102 [7]; cognition h²≈0.25 [22]). The quantified residual overlap bias was ≤3% of the effect magnitude under the assumed same-direction observational scenarios and did not alter significance; however, because the estimator bounds magnitude but not direction, this supports—but does not establish—that the MCP→cognition association is little-affected by sample overlap.
5. **NHANES** 2011–2018 cycles do not contain a chronic-pain variable that overlaps with the cognitive-assessment module in the same respondents; the descriptive memory-complaint correlates are reported in Supplementary Note 1 only.
6. **CHARLS** longitudinal analysis is now reported (§3.5) and extended to four waves as a sensitivity analysis (§3.5.1). Residual limitations: the 2011–2015 cognition follow-up spans only four years with strong practice effects, limiting power for decline detection; the 2018 cognition module uses a *different, easier* 12-word list and a single-field serial-7, making it non-comparable to earlier waves — when naively added it induces a cohort-level cognition jump (cross-sample z +0.919 at 2018) that produces a spurious *positive* pain×time LMM interaction (β=+0.0015, p=0.012), which we disclose as a measurement artefact rather than a true effect (§3.5.1); pain was self-reported; BMI was not available in the extracted modules and could not be adjusted; and the global cognition composite excluded orientation/drawing items (scoring requires per-respondent interview dates). These constrain but do not invalidate the cross-sectional dose–response finding or the null incident-impairment hazard (both three- and four-wave Cox p>0.38); the orientation/drawing omission, which comprises the most dementia-sensitive MMSE items, likely further attenuates any true longitudinal signal, so the null Cox is a conservative test of decline. A change-score (ANCOVA) sensitivity additionally produced a divergent, baseline-conditioned pain→decline signal (§3.5.2) that we attribute to regression-to-the-mean rather than true decline. Depression (CESD) was not included as a covariate in the LMM/Cox models despite being named in the Introduction as a profound confounder; the cross-sectional dose–response direction signal may therefore be partly confounded by depression and should be read as hypothesis-generating rather than independent confirmation.
7. **Outlier robustness** — MR-PRESSO / formal outlier re-estimation (specified in the SAP) could not be run because TwoSampleMR was unavailable; leave-one-out was used as the primary proxy. With I² 83.5% and the weighted median (−0.949) diverging from and exceeding the fixed-effect IVW estimate (−0.361, lying outside its 95% CI), this is a binding limitation on horizontal-pleiotropy inference: leave-one-out detects only single-SNP influence, and the weighted-median's >50%-valid assumption is untested at this heterogeneity, so the 'robust primary' designation is correspondingly down-weighted.
8. **Multiple testing, ancestry, and power** — no formal correction was applied (all p-values reported); all GWAS are European-ancestry, so estimates are European-specific and generalisation to other ancestries requires caution (CHARLS, Chinese, will permit a cross-ancestry check once available); the null dementia/AD and mediation results may reflect limited power of binary/distant outcomes rather than true nulls.
9. **Endpoint interpretation.** The headline outcome (`ebi-a-GCST006572`) is a **single-time-point, UKB-derived cognitive-performance score dominated by fluid (verbal–numeric) reasoning**, not a measure of within-person cognitive *decline* and not a clinical diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or dementia (§2.2). The MR estimate β=−0.361 should therefore be read as a 1-SD lower genetically predicted score per 1-SD higher MCP liability — an effect on measured performance level, not on decline rate or on diagnostic conversion. Claims about "cognitive decline" or "dementia risk" are supported only by the (inconsistent) dementia/AD outcomes and the CHARLS component, and not by the headline estimate.
10. **Clinical magnitude.** Both effect estimates are modest. The MR estimate is −0.361 SD of cognitive performance per **1-SD** higher genetically predicted MCP liability; a 1-SD shift in a continuous liability score is large, so the performance difference associated with more modest, clinically realistic contrasts in pain burden is proportionally smaller still. In CHARLS, the observed per-site cross-sectional effect (−0.011 SD) means that even three additional painful sites correspond to ≈0.11 SD of global cognition (0 to ≥3 sites, per the §3.5 RCS). The much larger and longer UKB cohort reports a per-site HR of 1.08 for incident dementia [1], which is not directly comparable to the present CHARLS Cox estimate of 1.006 for incident worst-quartile cognitive impairment over a 4-year window. They do not support individual-level prognostic claims, and no minimally important difference has been established for this endpoint.

11. **Pain phenotype granularity.** The Johnston et al. (2019) MCP GWAS treats the count of painful body sites as a continuous liability, but does not distinguish neuropathic from nociceptive pain, nor pain intensity, duration beyond the ≥3-month threshold, or temporal pattern. Different pain phenotypes have different mechanistic links to cognition — neuropathic pain involves direct central sensitisation and white-matter changes; nociceptive pain acts more via somatic symptom burden — so the SNP-instrumented 'pain-site count' should be interpreted as an aggregate liability rather than a mechanistically homogeneous exposure. Future MR with neuropathic-pain-specific GWAS would help disentangle this.

12. **Single- vs multisite generalisability (STROBE-MR 17c).** We did not disaggregate multisite from single-site chronic pain: the MCP exposure is defined as pain at ≥2 sites, so these findings may not generalise to single-site pain, and the dose–response below the multisite threshold is unexamined.

## 5. Conclusion
In a bidirectional two-sample MR framework with formal overlap-bias quantification and an independent Chinese longitudinal cohort, genetically predicted multisite chronic pain is associated with lower cognitive performance at the population level, but the effect is small in magnitude, the causal direction is unresolved, and the longitudinal clinical-decline signal in CHARLS is null. The clinical interpretation is that multisite chronic pain is a marker of population-level cognitive vulnerability rather than an individually prognostic factor for cognitive decline or dementia. Genetic evidence is compatible with an association between genetically predicted multisite chronic pain and lower cognitive performance, but the causal direction is uncertain (significant reverse MR; non-significant Steiger) and the cognitive-performance result is constrained by UKB sample overlap between exposure and outcome. A non-UKB overlap-free re-framing (FinnGen dementia; IGAP AD — the same estimates as §3.1, re-framed as overlap-free) pointed in the same clinical direction as the UKB cognition result and the IGAP AD estimate was significant, but because it re-uses the §3.1 estimates it does not provide independent evidence against UKB overlap; all-cause dementia was non-significant and replication is needed. No mediator was identified and the mechanism is unknown. These findings justify, but do not yet establish, multisite chronic pain as a marker of population-level cognitive vulnerability rather than a modifiable contributor to cognitive health; the overlap bias was bounded at ≤3% of effect magnitude under the assumed scenarios (§3.1.2) and found not to alter the conclusion. The CHARLS longitudinal component is now included and corroborates the association's direction with a dose–response signal, while its longitudinal decline evidence remains limited by the short, practice-effect-dominated follow-up (a four-wave sensitivity extension including the 2018 cognition module again yielded a null incident-impairment hazard, HR 1.005, p=0.381). A change-score (ANCOVA) sensitivity produced a nominally significant but methodologically divergent pain→decline signal that does not alter this conclusion (§3.5.2).
